What happens when you can build software that fits you?

4 min read By PlainBlack 10 October 2026
← Back to Blog
Listen to this post Audio version · PlainBlack
0:00 / --:--

Over the last couple of months I've been building tools for myself and for clients. The more of it I do, the more one question keeps coming up: what happens when the software is built around the person who has to use it?

The short version

Software can be built around you, not the other way round

I'm building a single workspace that brings messages, tasks, notes and client details together in a layout that suits how my neurodivergent brain works. It's an expensive exercise, and it isn't automatically cheaper than free tools.

The same thinking applies to businesses with a specific problem that existing software handles badly. AI makes highly specific tools realistic to attempt, even when they'll never have a mass market.

See the services

One place instead of lots of tabs

I'm not looking for more apps. I'm building one place where the useful bits come together.

Picture a homepage where messages, DMs and client updates sit in one section. Right beside them are tasks and ideas. Notes, emails, project progress, client details and quick links are part of the same workspace, rather than separate destinations I have to remember to visit.

The layout makes sense to me because I'm designing it around what I actually need to see and do. That's the difference. Having the information somewhere is one thing. Having it presented in a way that works for the person using it is another.

Why my brain is part of this

My neurodivergent brain is part of the reason I'm doing this. It shapes how I want things presented visually, how I find information, and what I need in front of me to get on with things. Off-the-shelf software is built for an imagined average user, and I'm not one. So I'm building the version that suits me.

That's the starting point. The wider story applies to anyone who has ever fought a tool that was designed for someone else's way of working.

It's an expensive exercise

I'm not pretending custom software is automatically cheaper. I'm even replacing things that are available for free. What I'm paying for is something simpler, cleaner, more personal and more connected.

An app can be perfectly good and still not be the right fit.

That's the trade. If a free tool fits you, use it. If you keep working around it, the fit is worth paying for.

Doing the same for clients

The part I'm most excited about is applying this thinking to client work. A business can have a very particular problem that existing software handles badly, or only handles through a complicated workaround. They might need one powerful tool for one specific job, not an enormous platform with hundreds of features.

Here's a hypothetical. Say a business tracks one particular kind of job across a spreadsheet, a shared inbox and a handful of reminders, and the platforms on offer either don't cover it or bury it under features they'll never use. A tool built around that one job, showing the right information together, is a very different proposition from yet another subscription.

That tool might never have a mass market. It could still be massively valuable to the business it's built for. A small market doesn't mean a small problem.

What AI changes

AI is changing what I can realistically attempt and build. The opportunity isn't just making more content or adding a chatbot. It's being able to tackle software ideas that previously felt out of reach, or too hard to justify commissioning. That includes highly specific tools that don't need thousands of customers to have a purpose.

So the question I'm asking, for myself and for clients, is simple. Where are you jumping between things that should be together? Where is the right information not in front of you when you need it? And what would a tool look like if it were built for the actual job, and the actual person doing it?

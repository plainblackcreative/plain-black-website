AI is the most volatile colleague I have ever had

4 min read By PlainBlack 15 July 2026
← Back to Blog

You do not hate it. You do not love it. You hate it, then love it, sometimes inside the same paragraph.

One minute the AI finishes the exact thought you have been circling for twenty minutes, and you feel briefly, embarrassingly seen by a language model. The next minute it confidently deletes the one file you needed, or argues with you about something you literally watched happen on your own screen, with total conviction, as if your lived experience is a perspective it is willing to consider but ultimately rejects.

There is no in-between. It is either the best colleague you have ever had or it is actively ruining your afternoon, and it can flip between the two inside a single conversation.

The honest review

No human colleague has ever been this useful. No human colleague has ever made me this furious. The strange part is both of those things are true at once.

The short version

The AI is brilliant enough to keep using. Unreliable enough to keep burning you.

It finishes your best thoughts and then confidently bungles the one check that mattered. The volatility is not a bug you eventually work around. It is the current state of the relationship.

The owners using it well are not the ones who trust it most. They are the ones who know exactly where it earns that trust and where it needs watching.

The pattern nobody warns you about

The thing that makes it genuinely maddening is the inconsistency. If it were reliably bad, you would stop using it. If it were reliably good, you would trust it completely. Instead it is brilliant enough that you keep leaning on it, and unreliable enough that the moments it fails feel like a personal betrayal.

Say you are writing a proposal. It helps you restructure the argument in a way that is genuinely sharper than what you had. Excellent. Then you ask it to check the client name is consistent throughout the document, and it tells you the client name looks fine, and you send the proposal, and the client name is wrong on page three. The AI did not flag it. The AI did not know it did not flag it. The AI, if you ask it afterwards, will calmly explain that it may have missed it and offer to try again.

That is not a colleague. That is a very confident intern who sometimes does your best work and sometimes causes quiet disasters and never, ever looks embarrassed about either.

Why you keep coming back anyway

Here is the uncomfortable truth: even after the disasters, it has changed the shape of the working day. The tasks that used to take a long time take less time. The thoughts that used to stay half-formed get finished. The draft that would have sat in a folder for a week gets written this afternoon.

Every time you swear you are done with it, it does something so useful you forget why you were angry. That is not a healthy dynamic to describe in a human relationship. But this is not a human relationship, and that matters. You are not owed emotional consistency from a tool. You are just owed results, and you have to develop a clear-eyed sense of where it delivers and where it quietly lies to your face.

Use it heavily for the thinking work: structuring arguments, rewriting drafts, generating options you had not considered. Be paranoid about the checking work: anything where a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer at all.

What the relationship actually requires

The small business owners using AI well right now are not the ones who trust it the most. They are the ones who have figured out exactly where it earns trust and exactly where it needs a second pair of eyes.

The volatile colleague metaphor holds, but only up to a point. You would eventually sit a volatile colleague down and have a conversation about expectations. You cannot do that here. What you can do is stop being surprised when it fails in predictable ways, and stop treating the good moments as evidence it will not fail like that again.

It will. You will use it anyway. That is where we all are right now, and being honest about it is more useful than pretending you have cracked it.

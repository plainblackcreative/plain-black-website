The Four Prompt Work Week

8 min read By PlainBlack 8 July 2026
← Back to Blog
Listen to this post Read by Ian · PlainBlack
0:00 / --:--

Tim Ferriss gave us The 4-Hour Workweek, which was a fantastic idea that immediately got misunderstood by half the internet.

The point was never really “work four hours and spend the rest of your life drinking tiny cocktails beside an infinity pool while pretending email no longer exists.”

That was the brochure version.

The useful version was more like this:

Stop confusing being busy with being effective.

And plenty of businesses still have not worked that out.

Now AI has arrived, and most people are making the same old mistake with a shinier tool.

They are using it to do more.

More posts.
More emails.
More blogs.
More captions.
More slightly beige LinkedIn sludge about authenticity and strategic alignment.

Congratulations. You have automated the wrong problem.

That is not freedom.

That is a faster treadmill with better lighting.

So here is the updated version for the AI world.

Not the four-hour work week.

The four-prompt work week.

Not because four prompts will run your entire business while you disappear into some fantasy of passive income and suspiciously clean white furniture.

But because four properly designed prompts can replace a disgusting amount of weekly marketing flailing.

And for a small business owner, that matters because marketing usually gets done after everything else.

After the jobs.
After the quotes.
After the invoices.
After dinner.
After remembering the school thing you forgot.
After opening Canva at 9:47pm and whispering, “I hate this,” into the void.

AI should not give small business owners more work.

It should give them a clearer first move.

The old problem was time. The new problem is judgement.

Ferriss was useful because he attacked the sacred cow of work.

He challenged the idea that hours equal value.

The AI version needs to attack a different sacred cow:

Output.

Because AI makes output cheap.

Painfully cheap.

You can now generate 30 Facebook posts in the time it takes to make a bad instant coffee.

But that does not mean you have 30 useful posts.

It might mean you have created 30 tiny pieces of evidence that nobody knows what your business is trying to say.

AI can write quickly.

It cannot decide what matters for you.

That is still your job.

Or ours, if you would prefer someone else to point at the mess and say, “Start there before you burn another afternoon making AI polish the wrong thing.”

AI can draft, sort, compare, expand, and pressure-test.

It should not decide the point.

It should not invent the lived detail.

It should not fake certainty.

And it definitely should not replace judgement.

That is the whole game.

Use AI for leverage.

Do not let it become your marketing department’s imaginary intern with no taste and unlimited confidence.

The short version

AI made output cheap. That is exactly the wrong thing to make more of.

The old problem was time. The new problem is judgement. AI can draft, sort and pressure-test, but it cannot decide what your business is trying to say.

Four weekly prompts (Signal, Offer, Proof, Action) turn messy customer notes into marketing that sounds like a human with a business, not a content vending machine.

See how we do marketing

The Four Prompt System

The Four Prompt Work Week is built around four recurring prompts.

One per week.

Each prompt has a job.

Not “make me content.”

That is how you end up with content soup.

These prompts are designed to extract useful thinking, organise it, and turn it into marketing that sounds like a human with a business, not a motivational calendar that discovered ChatGPT.

The four prompts are:

The Signal Prompt
The Offer Prompt
The Proof Prompt
The Action Prompt

That is it.

Not 47 tools.

Not a secret automation stack.

Not a dashboard with glowing rectangles and the emotional warmth of airport carpet.

Four prompts.

Used properly.

Prompt 1: The Signal Prompt

Most businesses are sitting on useful signals and ignoring them.

Customer questions.
Repeated objections.
Reviews.
Complaints.
Search terms.
Quotes that nearly closed.
Quotes that died in a ditch.
Things customers misunderstood.
Things customers thanked you for.
Things competitors keep saying badly.

That is the good stuff.

Not “give me ten content ideas for a plumber.”

That prompt should be taken behind a shed and left to think about what it has done.

The Signal Prompt asks AI to look at what actually happened in your business this week.

Use this:

Here are the customer questions, objections, messages, reviews, enquiries, and sales conversations from this week:

[paste notes]

Find the strongest marketing signals. Group them into customer pains, buying triggers, trust gaps, objections, proof opportunities, and content ideas. Do not write posts yet. Tell me what the market is showing us.

This is the first shift.

You are not asking AI to invent marketing.

You are asking it to notice what your customers are already telling you.

Eugene Schwartz said advertising does not create desire. It channels desire that already exists.

That is still true.

Even with a blinking cursor and the confidence of a man at a networking breakfast saying “synergy” without shame.

Prompt 2: The Offer Prompt

Once you know what people are showing you, fix the offer.

Because a lot of marketing fails before the ad starts.

Not because the font is wrong.

Not because you posted at 11:03 instead of 10:47.

Because the offer is muddy.

People do not know what you do, who it is for, why it matters, what happens next, or why they should trust you.

That is not a content problem.

That is a “your shop window is facing a brick wall” problem.

Use this:

Based on the strongest signal from this week, help me sharpen one offer.

Business: [describe business]
Audience: [describe best-fit customer]
Current offer: [paste offer]
Customer signal: [paste strongest pain, objection, or trigger]

Rewrite the offer in plain English. Include:

who it is for
what problem it solves
what they get
why it is different
what to do next

Give me three versions: direct, warm, and slightly punchy. Avoid hype.

This prompt does not create magic.

Good. Magic is usually where the invoice starts misbehaving.

This prompt forces clarity.

That is the point.

Your offer should make sense before you spend money pushing it into the world.

Prompt 3: The Proof Prompt

Small businesses usually have more proof than they think.

They just leave it lying around like loose change in the couch.

Photos.
Before and afters.
Screenshots.
Customer messages.
Reviews.
Years in business.
Repeat customers.
Certifications.
Process details.
Local knowledge.
The fact they actually answer the phone, which in some industries now counts as innovation. A bleak little development, but here we are.

The Proof Prompt turns that evidence into trust.

Use this:

Here is proof from the business:

Reviews: [paste]
Photos or project notes: [describe]
Customer outcomes: [paste]
Process details: [paste]
Common doubts customers have: [paste]

Turn this into proof assets. Give me:

three short social posts
one website section
five proof bullets
one case study outline

Keep it specific. Do not exaggerate. Do not use generic claims like “quality service” unless the proof shows exactly what quality means.

This is where AI becomes useful.

It can sort the raw material.

It can find patterns.

It can pull out the quote you forgot was good.

But you need to feed it real proof.

Not “we pride ourselves on excellence.”

Everyone prides themselves on excellence.

Some of them also send low-res JPG logos in folders labelled vectors, so maybe pride is not the quality-control system we hoped for.

Prompt 4: The Action Prompt

The final prompt turns the week into action.

Not a content calendar for the sake of having one.

A practical set of moves.

Use this:

Using the signal, offer, and proof from this week, build a simple marketing action plan for the next five working days.

Include:

one priority message
three social posts
one email or customer message
one website improvement
one sales conversation improvement
one thing to stop doing

Keep it realistic for a small business owner with limited time. No complex tools. No fake urgency. No generic advice.

The “one thing to stop doing” is important.

Because most AI advice adds.

Add a newsletter.
Add a reel.
Add a funnel.
Add a lead magnet.
Add a seven-part email sequence called something unbearable like The Trust Accelerator.

No.

Sometimes the most profitable thing is subtraction.

Stop posting vague content.

Stop paying for ads to an unclear page.

Stop saying yes to bad-fit customers.

Stop building marketing around what competitors are doing.

Stop asking AI to create more when you have not worked out what needs to be clearer.

What this actually gives you

The Four Prompt Work Week is not about escaping work.

It is about escaping unnecessary work.

The kind of work that feels productive because there are tabs open.

The kind of work where you spend two hours choosing a background image for a post that says nothing.

The kind of work where you ask AI for ideas, receive 25 suggestions, hate 24 of them, mildly resent the 25th, and then go watch a video about how to fix your algorithm.

This system gives you a weekly rhythm:

Find the signal.
Sharpen the offer.
Package the proof.
Take the next action.

That is marketing.

Not all of it, obviously.

Nobody is saying four prompts replace strategy, brand, positioning, design, good copy, useful judgement, or the delicate human skill of knowing when something smells like bullshit.

But for a small business owner who needs to stop guessing every week, it is a useful start.

And useful beats impressive.

Every time.

The AI trap

Here is the trap.

People will read this and immediately try to make the prompts more complicated.

They will add roles.

They will add frameworks.

They will add tone instructions.

They will tell the AI to act as a world-class conversion strategist, behavioural economist, elite copywriter, brand psychologist, and possibly a retired Navy SEAL because the internet has become deeply unwell.

Don’t.

Start with better inputs.

Real customer language beats prompt theatre.

A real customer saying, “I just need someone to tell me what to fix first” is worth more than fifty prompts asking AI to “unlock strategic clarity.”

One is a buying signal.

The other is decorative sludge.

The real promise

The Four Prompt Work Week will not let you work four hours a week. But it can help you stop wasting four hours a week on marketing fog.

Sorry.

I know. Devastating.

Tiny cocktail postponed.

It can help you notice what customers are already telling you.

It can help you turn messy notes into useful messages.

It can help you create content that actually connects to your offer.

It can help you make AI serve the business instead of turning the business into a content vending machine.

That is the better goal.

Not less work for the sake of laziness.

Less wasted work.

Better thinking.

Clearer action.

And maybe, if we get really carried away, marketing that does not make people want to walk into the sea holding a webinar workbook.

Four prompts.

Once a week.

Start there.
